background

Russian currency control regulations have historically been both complicated and strict, with complex rules and severe penalties for non-compliance.

Since 2022, meanwhile, the challenging geopolitical situation has led to some relaxation of these regulations, enforcement has become more stringent (e.g., currency control violations identified in 2024 numbered 118,947, which is 60% more than in 2023).

Furthermore, since 2022, so-called 'counter-sanctions' regulations have been developed in response to economic pressure from Western states. This framework, which is constantly being updated, introduces new restrictions on top of standard currency regulations, particularly in relation to transfer of funds or other assets to non-residents. These new restrictions apply primarily (but not exclusively) to persons from or controlled by 'unfriendly' countries.

In this article, we concentrate on the general currency control regulations, not the constantly evolving counter-sanctions regulations.

legal framework

The key law governing currency operations in Russia is Federal Law No. 173-FZ dated 10 December 2003, also known as the 'Currency Control Law', which sets the fundamental currency regulation and control rules in Russia.

To enforce the Currency Control Law in practice, the Central Bank of Russia (CBR) and the Russian Government issue numerous regulatory acts.

CBR Instruction No. 181-I dated 16 August 2017 stands out among these as the key document establishing the obligations to prepare and submit documents in order to ensure currency control compliance.

authorities and agents

The currency control authorities in Russia are the CBR, the Russian tax and customs authorities (specifically when goods are moved across the customs border). These authorities conduct audits of compliance with the currency laws and can impose penalties on violators independently or, in certain cases (e.g., significant breaches), refer relevant materials for consideration by the courts.

Additionally, Russian banks and other professional market participants (brokers, depositories, etc.) act as currency control 'agents' by monitoring compliance with the currency laws and passing the relevant information to the currency control authorities.

residents and non-residents

The Currency Control Law draws a distinction between Russian residents and non-residents. Different rules apply to each group.

Russian residents include

  • Russian legal entities (among them those controlled by foreign persons)
  • Representative offices (branches) of Russian legal entities outside Russia
  • Russian citizens and individuals with a permanent residence permit in Russia

Non-residents include

  • Foreign legal entities
  • Representative offices (branches) of foreign legal entities in Russia
  • Individuals who do not qualify as residents

foreign trade and loans for non-residents

export of goods, works and services

Historically, Russian currency control laws required Russian residents to repatriate from non-residents funds payable for exported goods, works and services. This meant that Russian residents had to ensure that the relevant funds were received from their counterparties into an account in a Russian bank, there being no alternative options for settling obligations (e.g., through offsetting).

The repatriation rule has since been relaxed in relation to sale of certain goods, works and services, including ones paid for in Russian rubles (₽). In addition, the repatriation requirement was temporarily suspended in 2022 owing to practical difficulties in making international payments.

Despite the suspension of the repatriation requirement, Russian residents must still ensure that the obligations of their non-resident counterparties are either duly fulfilled (e.g., through payment) or terminated (e.g., by being offset or alternative consideration being provided).

Failing to ensure these obligations are either fulfilled or terminated could result in liability (see more below).

NB! Russian law provides a 45-day grace period, so there is no liability if a violation is remedied within this term.

Practical example A

Scenario: In February 2025, a Russian company (RusCo) provided IT services to a Hong Kong company (HKCo). Under the contract, payment was due by 31 March 2025.

Issue: HKCo did not make the payment by the deadline.

Consequences: RusCo has to obtain the payment or ensure other settlement of the payment obligation by 31 March 2025. Given the 45-day grace period, if RusCo has not received the funds or ensured alternative settlement by 15 May 2025, RusCo and its officials might be subject to liability.

import of goods, works and services

Under the Currency Control Law, if a Russian resident makes an advance payment to a non-resident for imported goods, works or services, it must either ensure the goods are delivered or the relevant payment is returned. This rule prevents funds from being sent out of the country without a corresponding return.

Practical example B

Scenario: On 28 February 2025, a Russian purchaser (RusCo) transferred an advance payment for equipment to a Hong Kong supplier (HKCo). The equipment was to be delivered by 31 March 2025.

Issue: The equipment was not delivered within the specified period, nor did HKCo return the advance payment.

Consequences: If HKCo does not deliver the equipment or return the money by 15 May 2025 (i.e., 31 March 2025 + a 45-day grace period), RusCo and its officials might face administrative liability.

granting of loans to non-residents

Just as in the case of export of certain goods, works and services, loans granted by Russian residents to non-residents were previously subject to funds having to be repatriated to Russia.

Accordingly, although the requirement to repatriate funds was temporarily suspended in 2022, Russian residents are still legally responsible for ensuring that the obligations under loan agreements with non-residents are either duly fulfilled or terminated.

Practical example C

Scenario: On 28 February 2025, a Russian company (RusCo) granted a loan to a foreign company (HKCo) under a loan agreement. The loan was due to be repaid by 31 March 2025.

Issue: The loan was not repaid by HKCo.

Consequences: RusCo has to obtain payment or otherwise settle the payment obligation. Given the 45-day grace period, if RusCo has not received the funds or settled the payment obligation by 15 May 2025, RusCo and its officials might face administrative liability.

foreign trade and loans for non-residents

Russian residents engaging in foreign trade (export/import of goods, works, services), as well as granting loans to non-residents, must register their contracts with their servicing Russian bank if the contract value exceeds certain thresholds. For instance:

  • ₽ 3m (approx. ¥ 265k) for import, services and loans, and
  • ₽ 10m (approx. ¥ 883k) for other types of contract.

As currency control agents, Russian banks require residents to provide supporting documents for all cross-border transactions (contracts, invoices, customs declarations, etc.).

Residents must also submit regular reports (e.g., certificates of foreign currency operations, certificates of supporting documents, etc.) to their servicing bank.

penalties for non-compliance

If a Russian resident fails to comply with currency control laws, they may face administrative liability (fines). The offences fall into two groups:

Reporting Violations: These are typically minor offences involving failure to submit the requisite reports and documents to the bank on time. The relevant fines are relatively small, usually a few thousand yuan.

For example, failure by a resident to submit to the servicing bank full reports on foreign exchange transactions within 90 days of the established deadline could entail a fine of ₽ 40–50k (~ ¥ 3.5–4.4k).

Failure to Fulfil Obligations: These are more serious offences involving failure to comply with the repatriation requirement, fulfil or settle obligations under contracts (in the absence of repatriation requirements), or return funds for goods, works or services not delivered to Russia.

For example, if the unfulfilled or terminated obligations (non-repatriated funds) are less than ₽ 10m, the fine will be 5–30% of the sum. If the amount exceeds ₽ 10m, the fine increases significantly to a range 75–100%. Company officials might also be held administratively liable, becoming subject to not very significant fines comparable to those specified in 'penalties' above.

Even so, a more serious risk for officials is administrative liability in the form of up to three years' disqualification from holding management positions. In the worst-case scenario, company officials might even face criminal liability for the violations specified in 'penalties' above. Such cases are rare in practice but the theoretical possibility should be taken into consideration.

practical tips

maintaining good working relations with the bank

Given the banks' role in monitoring compliance, it is advisable for Russian residents to establish and maintain good working relations with their servicing bank. So, if the bank notices a potential issue with a transaction, they might warn the company in advance, helping to preclude a violation and potential penalties.

demonstrating diligence

When assessing potential liability, regulators and courts often consider whether the resident has taken proactive steps, such as sending a demand letter or initiating legal proceedings. If the resident can demonstrate that they have done everything possible to fulfil their obligations, they may be exempted from liability or have it reduced, even if a technical violation has taken place.

take preemptive action

If you foresee a high probability of a violation for any reason, especially objective factors (e.g., difficulties with cross-border payments), the best course of action is to be proactive. It might be better to negotiate an addendum with the counterparty to extend the deadline for fulfilling obligations rather than to risk a formal default.

be mindful of counter-sanctions

It is critical to exercise caution when dealing with a counterparty from or controlled by a company from an 'unfriendly' country. In such cases, it is important to review carefully whether the transaction or its performance complies with the complex and ever evolving counter-sanctions regulations.
