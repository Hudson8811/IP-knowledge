Russia enjoys a resilient economy, abundant natural resources and a growing consumer market. Withdrawal by Western brands has opened up a rare opportunity for Chinese companies to expand into sectors ranging from consumer goods and e-commerce to technology, infrastructure and energy. Today, Chinese enterprises benefit on the Russian market from being seen as partners from a ‘friendly’ state, this granting them advantages unavailable to companies from the West. While Chinese businesses enjoy substantial opportunities, the Russian legal and regulatory landscape is complex and constantly evolving.

This article provides a general overview of the key legal considerations for Chinese companies dealing with Russia and (or) doing business in the country. As a leading Russian full-service law firm, Ivanyan and Partners is here to provide the practical guidance needed for navigating this intricate landscape with confidence at every step of your business journey.

current trends

Import substitution and production localisation: Russian businesses actively seek technological cooperation and investment from friendly countries in order to develop local production capabilities, with Chinese partners being welcomed as suppliers of machinery and technology, and as investors in local manufacturing.

Growing Cooperation with China: in recent years, China has become Russia’s No. 1 trading partner, the two countries’ bilateral trade surpassing $240 billion in 2024 and their national currencies (¥ and ₽) being used for over 90% of trade settlements.

State Investment Support: 140+ special economic zones (SEZ) offer tax holidays, customs preferences, reduced social security contributions and other incentives; Chinese companies are increasingly visible among SEZ residents.

sanctions read full article

western restrictions

Russia has been successfully resisting the vast number of economic restrictions (sanctions) imposed by ‘unfriendly’ states.

  • The most comprehensive sanctions regimes have been introduced by the USA and the EU, including blocking sanctions in relation to a number of Russian individuals (i.e., asset freezing) and sectoral sanctions (i.e., bans on new investment, financing, securities trading, etc. in certain sectors of the Russian economy).
  • The sanctions are concentrated on strategic sectors of the Russian economy: defence, energy and finance, plus companies and people connected with them.

counter-sanctions

Russia has responded with a vast number of special economic measures (counter-sanctions) imposing additional limitations and government clearance requirements on certain operations involving persons connected with ‘unfriendly’ states.

‘Unfriendly states’ are the USA, the UK, the EU member states, Singapore and popular offshore jurisdictions (the BVI, Bahamas, Cayman Islands, etc.). The full list is maintained by the Russian Government.*

‘Unfriendly’ persons are considered to be:

  • persons/companies from ‘unfriendly’ countries, and
  • companies from ‘friendly’ countries controlled by persons from ‘unfriendly’ countries (e.g., through more than 50% ownership).

NB! The People’s Republic of China is not deemed an ’unfriendly’ state and the counter-sanctions do not apply to: Chinese persons or companies, unless they are controlled by ‘unfriendly’ persons, and ‘unfriendly’ companies controlled by Chinese (and other ‘friendly’ persons) since 01.03.2022.

Practical tip: To preclude numerous practical problems, when dealing with Russia, it is better to avoid using companies registered in ‘unfriendly’ jurisdictions (e.g., use Hong Kong instead of Singapore, etc.)

the Russian legal framework

the legal system

  • belongs to the continental family (statutory law is more important than court precedent);
  • regional legislation might include certain specifics compared to the federal legislation (land laws, administrative fines, etc.);
  • generally in line with modern international practices (substantial reforms in the mid-2010s introduced the concepts of warranties, indemnity, option agreements, etc., accompanied by a major arbitration reform).

key forms of local presence

Subsidiary company: the most popular form is a limited liability company (LLC): minimum charter capital — ₽10k (~¥880), must be paid within 4 months of registration, time to set up: 3–6 weeks.

Counter-sanctions clearance is required if set up by an ‘unfriendly’ person.

Representative office (RO)/branch: not an independent legal entity; the head of an RO/branch acts on the basis of a Power of Attorney from the ‘parent’ company; time required for setting up: 8–12 weeks.

No counter-sanctions clearance is required if set up by an ‘unfriendly’ person. A subsidiary company is typically used for trade and investment projects, while ROs and branches are often preferred for managing relations with regulators and clients.

Practical tip: There is no legally recognised nominal shareholder/director concept. A real person would usually be required to represent the company/RO/branch before the authorities and 3rd parties.

The business registration process is generally straightforward, involving choosing a legal form, preparing documents, submitting them to the tax authority, obtaining registration documents and taking post-registration steps (opening a bank account, etc.).

government clearance

Establishment of a local company/purchase of shares in an existing one might require government clearance:

  • Antitrust Clearance (from the Federal Antimonopoly Service) might be required for incorporation/acquisition of shares in a company in excess of certain monetary thresholds;
  • Foreign Investment Clearance (from the Government Commission on Monitoring Foreign Investment) might be required for a foreign state or foreign state-owned entity to acquire shares in a Russian company;
  • Strategic Company Clearance (from the Government Commission on Monitoring Foreign Investment) might be required for acquisition of 25%+ of the shares in an existing company carrying out ’strategic activities’ (e.g., fishing, subsoil exploration/mining, nuclear and space activities, etc.);
  • Countersanctions Clearance (from the Government Commission Subcommittee / President of the Russian Federation) might be required for incorporation/acquisition of company shares (if a person from an ‘unfriendly’ jurisdiction is involved).

NB! Banks, insurance, leasing and other strictly regulated industries could require additional approvals.

currency control read full article

  • Russian ruble (₽) is the same currency for both onshore and offshore operations.
  • Chinese companies are free to open accounts with Russian banks in any available currencies (e.g., ₽/¥).
  • Payments for goods involving a foreign company may be made in ₽, ¥, $ or other currencies (though payments between two Russian companies must be in ₽).
  • Owing to the sanctions, there are certain practical difficulties in making payments in $, €, £ and a number of other currencies.

No significant currency control restrictions apply to Chinese companies trading with or investing in Russia and return on investments (unless ‘unfriendly’ persons are involved).

Equity financing may be in ¥, ₽ or another currency but needs to be fixed in ₽ at the current exchange rate. Debt financing and repayment can be in ¥, ₽ or another currency.

No restrictions apply to dividend payments by a Russian company to a Chinese company/individual (provided that the recipient does not qualify as an ‘unfriendly’ person).
